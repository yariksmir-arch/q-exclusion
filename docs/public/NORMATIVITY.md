# SS, Normativity and Agency

**Status: interpretive application conditional on Structural Sufficiency (SS).** Existing theories explain different aspects of function, evaluation and action; their compatibility with SS is not evidence that SS is proved. The contribution here is to distinguish their substantive questions from a demand for an independent ontological remainder. No ethical theory or mathematical result is derived. [Primary references](BIBLIOGRAPHY.md#normativity-and-agency) identify the sources and reading locations.

## Biological and psychological normativity

A heart’s impaired contribution to circulation, an organism’s inability to tolerate environmental change and a person’s experienced pain are different matters. They concern relations among functioning, conditions of life and experienced significance, rather than departures from a single numerical average.

Canguilhem’s “The Normal and the Pathological” (2008 translation) distinguishes statistical normality from the capacity to establish viable relations with a changing milieu. Mossio, Saborido and Moreno (2009) propose an organizational account: functional contributions help maintain the organization that produces and maintains their bearers. This supplies conditions for distinguishing function from an accidentally useful effect. Di Paolo’s enactive account (2005) distinguishes continued self-production from adaptive regulation: merely surviving a perturbation does not establish a capacity to detect and counter a deteriorating tendency. These are substantive philosophical proposals about biological organization, not consequences of SS or interchangeable definitions of life.

Pain and affect add experienced significance, learning and regulation. The IASP’s revised definition and notes (2020) distinguish pain from nociception, recognize biological, psychological and social influences, and do not treat inability to communicate as ruling out pain. Pain can disrupt functioning rather than serve an adaptive role. Balleine and Dickinson’s work on animal instrumental learning (1998) distinguishes learning action–outcome contingencies from learning an outcome’s incentive value. Learning what produces an outcome and learning whether to pursue it are different achievements; neither defines moral worth.

These accounts articulate success, failure and significance through specified dependencies. Adaptiveness, health, felt relief and moral goodness do not thereby become equivalent. A harmful institution can maintain itself successfully; its persistence supplies no moral endorsement.

## Moral origins and revision

Tomasello’s reconstruction (summarized in 2018) links moral psychology to interdependent collaboration, joint commitments, partner expectations and later group norms. Henrich and colleagues (2010, corrected 2011) investigate culturally variable fairness and punishment across fifteen populations. Their associations between experimental behaviour and institutional participation support investigating learned norms and social organization; they do not establish a unique evolutionary history or show that markets cause moral goodness. Feeling obligated, enforcing a demand and justifying it are distinct. Cooperation, social learning and institutions help explain the formation and persistence of norms, including norms that deserve criticism.

Dewey’s *Human Nature and Conduct* (1922) treats habits as socially sustained and deliberation as an exploration of competing courses of action. Institutions and education can change the conditions through which habits develop. Kitcher’s *The Ethical Project* (2011; author’s précis) offers a partly conjectural analytical history of responses to failures of altruism and a pragmatic account of improvement. He explicitly connects assessments of progress to substantive normative proposals; history alone does not dictate them.

On these approaches, moral error need not be formal contradiction. A coherent rule can be challenged for neglected suffering, excluded claims or unequal burdens. Such criticism needs reasons, including an account of why those considerations matter; consistency alone cannot settle it. Progress can mean correcting a specified failure and extending consideration without measuring distance from an absolute final scale. Whether a proposed change improves matters, whose standpoint was excluded and what losses it imposes remain disputable substantive questions. SS supplies neither their answer nor an endorsement of whatever a group accepts.

## Agency, freedom and responsibility

Fischer and Ravizza’s compatibilist account of responsibility (1998) develops guidance control through appropriately reasons-responsive mechanisms and their historically acquired ownership by an agent. This makes capacities and histories relevant without requiring self-creation independently of antecedent conditions. It is an existing, contested account of responsibility, not a theorem entailed by SS. Its possible-world formulations are not imported as foundational ontology.

Autonomy concerns self-government; coercion concerns how threats and constraints shape choices; learning changes dispositions and skills; control concerns capacities to guide action. Responsibility additionally concerns the grounds for attribution and the appropriateness of demands, praise, blame or repair. Someone can competently execute an action under coercion. Training can improve performance without establishing autonomy. A causal contribution alone does not settle blameworthiness.

Psychology and medicine investigate distinctions that a blanket label of “free” or “unfree” can conceal. Action–outcome learning differs from habitual responding. Corvol and colleagues’ longitudinal Parkinson disease study (2018) found associations between dopamine-agonist exposure and impulse-control disorders, including dose/duration relationships and improvement after discontinuation. This observational evidence makes treatment history relevant to understanding behavioural control; it establishes no verdict about a particular person’s responsibility. Medical impairment, moral appraisal and institutional accountability require different assessments. SS establishes neither universal determinism nor universal freedom.

## One case, several questions

Consider an employee who misses an agreed shift. The attendance record leaves open severe pain, a coercive threat, an avoidable oversight or a deliberate protest. Biological functioning, experienced distress, available alternatives, understanding of commitments and workplace arrangements bear on different assessments. An explanation of pain does not by itself justify the attendance rule; the rule’s coordinating function does not establish that sanctions are fair.

Applying the existing Q discipline, an account retaining only attendance cannot license distinctions that depend on these omitted circumstances. A richer account must supply and justify the relevant differences. Even then, assessing a demand to attend requires reasons about burdens, commitments and alternatives. Changing a rule to accommodate illness can redistribute work and create further conflicts. Neither adding a diagnosis nor identifying cooperation supplies an automatic moral answer.

## Interpretation under Q-exclusion and SS

The connection with [modality](MODALITY.md) is explanatory: capacities and supported alternatives have content through dependencies and constraints. Likewise, the accounts above explain functional standards, learned significance, mutual expectations and capacities for action through substantive organization. They need no separate nonstructural determinant to perform those explanatory tasks. Under SS, an additional normative fact stipulated to vary while substantive organization is fixed is excluded as an independent remainder. No current account is certified exhaustive.

The audit applies first to this application. Asking which norm is justified, whether suffering was overlooked or whether control was impaired requests substantive work. Missing explanations are not automatically Q-contamination. Nor may “organization” be defined as whatever guarantees a preferred moral verdict. A demand for an external moral arbiter, a special fact of “genuine bindingness” or an absolute author must specify its proposed contribution. The same scrutiny applies to demands for one boundary where normativity begins, an answer to every value conflict or a complete independent description of all relations. These demands do not follow from SS; arguments defending them still deserve assessment.

Q checks determination relative to supplied content, not ethical validity. SS adds conditional exclusion of an independent remainder, not a derivation of duties from value-free facts. It does not require reasons to be reconstructed from an exhaustive nonnormative description, and it does not refute moral realism generally. Whether particular reasons warrant demands, how norms should change and which responsibility practices are defensible remain philosophical and empirical work. This application organizes those tasks without replacing them.
